The takeaway

A hurricane lasts a few days. The ocean it feeds on has been warming for generations and warmer water means more moisture in the air, faster intensification, and heavier rain and storm surge when a storm finally reaches the coast.

Not every storm will be stronger. But each one now forms over an ocean we have quietly reshaped, charged by emissions that are global and that linger for centuries. The path we choose now decides how much more heat the ocean stores — and whether future generations inherit an Atlantic that stays closer to the low-emissions track, or keeps climbing toward the high one. Burn less, store less heat, and hurricanes form over water with less extra fuel waiting for them.

A hurricane is weather.
The warming ocean beneath it is climate.

Behind the data

Sources, methodology & limitations

Data sources

  • Atlantic sea surface temperature (long-term): Observed values from NOAA ERSST v5; future projections from CMIP6 GFDL-ESM4 under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5. (This series predates the satellite era and is shown as long-term context; it is a separate source from the GOES data below.)
  • Satellite imagery & sea surface temperature (recent): NOAA GOES (Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellites), accessed via the NOAA Open Data on AWS registry.
  • Country CO₂ emissions (“Follow the Fuel” leaderboard): Our World in Data (which compiles the Global Carbon Project and population data). Processed offline into a compact JSON of annual, cumulative, and per-capita emissions for real countries, 1850–present.

Methodology

The long-term chart shows annual-average Atlantic sea surface temperature (observed, then projected). The satellite views come from NOAA GOES-16: the sea surface temperature map uses the ABI-L2-SSTF product (quality-flagged, Kelvin converted to °C, composited over clear-sky days on 2022-09-20 and 2022-02-15 for seasonal comparison, reprojected to latitude/longitude and downsampled with a shared color scale), and the storm scene uses ABI-L2-MCMIPC visible, clean-infrared, and water-vapor bands for Hurricane Ian on 28 Sep 2022. All GOES data was processed offline in a notebook (process_goes.ipynb) into small JSON grids and WebP images, so this page loads quickly and does not depend on raw satellite files or any live data service. Because the sea surface is obscured by cloud beneath an active storm, the SST shown in the storm scene is a recent clear-sky composite that represents the warm water in the storm’s path. The “Make a hurricane” drag-map uses illustrative ingredient scores in data/hurricane_zones.json (not sampled forecasts); optional SST cells reuse the hurricane-season GOES grid above. The “Follow the Fuel” leaderboard loads data/emissions_country_year.json, built offline by scripts/process_emissions.py from the Our World in Data CO₂ dataset (real countries only; cumulative and per-capita derived where needed). It shows three metrics on purpose: annual emissions show current pressure, cumulative emissions show historical contribution, and per-person emissions show intensity relative to population — no single view is the whole story. Emissions are framed as contribution to the warming system, not as a direct cause of any individual storm.

Limitations

  • Hurricane damage depends on landfall location, population exposure, infrastructure, preparedness, and reporting — not on ocean temperature alone.
  • Historical damage comparisons are affected by inflation and coastal development, so they are not used here as a direct climate signal.
  • A correlation between climate variables and hurricane activity does not, by itself, prove direct causation.
  • GOES water-vapor imagery shows atmospheric moisture, not measured rainfall; it is not a precipitation total.
  • This project focuses on long-term environmental patterns, not on predicting any individual storm.
  • The “Make a hurricane” map uses illustrative zone scores (0–100) and a simplified educational model; it does not forecast storms, predict landfall, or calculate actual damage.
  • The game moisture map is a schematic field derived from zone scores; sea surface temperature uses a processed GOES snapshot when available (Sep 2022).
  • The “Follow the Fuel” leaderboard shows country CO₂ contribution to the warming climate system; it does not claim any country caused a specific hurricane. Annual, cumulative, and per-capita metrics each tell a different and incomplete story.
