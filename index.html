But

Warmth and moisture are not the whole story. What a storm does depends on where it lands, who lives there, and how prepared they are.

Why it reaches the shore

Destruction is built on the coast, not just at sea

Two storms with identical winds can leave wildly different damage behind. A powerful hurricane that stays offshore may harm no one; a weaker, slow-moving storm that stalls over a city can cause catastrophic flooding. Damage depends on landfall location, population exposure, infrastructure, drainage, and preparedness — human factors layered on top of the physical ones.

Therefore Hurricane risk is best understood as a combination: warming oceans and heavier moisture, storm intensity, and community vulnerability all aligning at once.

The takeaway

The clearest pattern isn’t stronger storms every year — it’s a changing environment

Year to year, hurricanes are noisy and unpredictable. The steadier signal is in the conditions they form in. The first chart showed an Atlantic that has warmed for over a century and may keep warming. The satellite views showed what that warmth and moisture look like beneath a real storm. Put together, they point to one idea: the environment hurricanes form in is shifting, and that can load storms with more destructive potential — making hurricane risk a long-term climate issue, not a single bad season.

Behind the data

Sources, methodology & limitations

Data sources

  • Atlantic sea surface temperature (long-term): Observed values from NOAA ERSST v5; future projections from CMIP6 GFDL-ESM4 under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5. (This series predates the satellite era and is shown as long-term context; it is a separate source from the GOES data below.)
  • Satellite imagery & sea surface temperature (recent): NOAA GOES (Geostationary Operational Environmental Satellites), accessed via the NOAA Open Data on AWS registry.

Methodology

The long-term chart shows annual-average Atlantic sea surface temperature (observed, then projected). The satellite views come from NOAA GOES-16: the sea surface temperature map uses the ABI-L2-SSTF product (quality-flagged, Kelvin converted to °C, composited over clear-sky days on 2022-09-20 and 2022-02-15 for seasonal comparison, reprojected to latitude/longitude and downsampled with a shared color scale), and the storm scene uses ABI-L2-MCMIPC visible, clean-infrared, and water-vapor bands for Hurricane Ian on 28 Sep 2022. All GOES data was processed offline in a notebook (process_goes.ipynb) into small JSON grids and WebP images, so this page loads quickly and does not depend on raw satellite files or any live data service. Because the sea surface is obscured by cloud beneath an active storm, the SST shown in the storm scene is a recent clear-sky composite that represents the warm water in the storm’s path. The “Make a hurricane” drag-map uses illustrative ingredient scores in data/hurricane_zones.json (not sampled forecasts); optional SST cells reuse the hurricane-season GOES grid above.

Limitations

  • Hurricane damage depends on landfall location, population exposure, infrastructure, preparedness, and reporting — not on ocean temperature alone.
  • Historical damage comparisons are affected by inflation and coastal development, so they are not used here as a direct climate signal.
  • A correlation between climate variables and hurricane activity does not, by itself, prove direct causation.
  • GOES water-vapor imagery shows atmospheric moisture, not measured rainfall; it is not a precipitation total.
  • This project focuses on long-term environmental patterns, not on predicting any individual storm.
  • The “Make a hurricane” map uses illustrative zone scores (0–100) and a simplified educational model; it does not forecast storms, predict landfall, or calculate actual damage.
  • The game moisture map is a schematic field derived from zone scores; sea surface temperature uses a processed GOES snapshot when available (Sep 2022).
